Living PRD (Product Requirements Document)

Updated every week as we test with real users and learn what works.

1. Target User & The Job to Be Done

Who is this for? Undergraduate and graduate students who are recruiting. First reachable users: my NYU Stern MBA classmates.

The painful "Before" workflow: Recruiting is overwhelming. Cold applying on LinkedIn rarely works anymore, so networking is now crucial. But preparing for each coffee chat or event takes 30+ minutes of scattered research, and contacts and follow-ups get lost in notes.

The "After" workflow with this product: The student sets career goals and target companies once and connects Google Calendar. For each networking event they get a 2-page prep brief, generated 48 hours before and emailed 24 hours before.

2. Scope & Explicit Non-Goals

  • In Scope (Core Workflow, v1): Profile (career goals + target companies) → Google Calendar events → 2-page prep brief → chat to ask follow-up questions or add info to the brief.
  • In Scope (Build 3): live app with Google login → calendar → researched brief with sources → follow-up with contacts (LinkedIn, approved emails) → Discover events. Daily automatic sync and a notetaker integration are still out.
  • In Scope (Build 2): Google login → Google Calendar (read-only) → networking filter → AI brief for one event, plus “Add event” for missed events. Email delivery and the follow-up chat are still simulated.
  • Out of Scope #1: No scraping LinkedIn or building profiles of people from web data. (When web search is added, only public professional information with a source link will be considered.)
  • Out of Scope #2: No job search or application tracking.
  • Out of Scope #3: No CV or keyword analysis.

What the 2-page prep brief contains (with links in every section):

  • Event, date, time and location
  • Person and role
  • Company overview
  • Relevant products
  • Latest financial results and highlights (if public)
  • 5 relevant company news items from the last 3 months
  • 5 relevant industry news items and why they matter
  • 10 suggested questions

Roadmap (after the core workflow):

  • Contacts list with company, role and last contact date.
  • Follow-up drafts (thank-you notes, to-dos).
  • AI notetaker integration (e.g. Granola) to keep a history of each conversation.
  • Industry news tab.
  • Recommended events (conferences, panels, recruiting events) based on the student's goals and target companies.
  • Recommended contacts based on materials the student uploads (event attendee lists, alumni directory exports), plus suggested roles to look for at each target company, with drafts to start the conversation.

3. How It Works Under the Hood & Riskiest Assumption (PRD v2, Build 2)

Riskiest assumption: a student's own calendar is enough to give a useful brief. That depends on three things: the app finds the right events, the events carry enough detail, and the AI has up-to-date information. If any of them fails, the brief is not worth opening.

  • Risk 1 · Missing or extra events. The classifier can miss a networking event or show one that is not networking. How Build 2 tests it: the classifier ran on my real calendar. Events I flagged as wrong became rules (recurring events, fellowship programs, weekly syncs, club coordination without a named guest), with tests on example titles. Mitigation now: an “Add event” button for missed events, and a type badge to correct any event. Next push: an option to remove events that are not relevant.
  • Risk 2 · Calendar events without enough detail. Many invites only have a title, so the AI does not know the company, the person or the topic. How Build 2 tests it: the brief now reads the event description and the company from attendee email domains, and says where each fact came from. Events with only a title still give thin briefs. Next push: define how the app asks the user for missing details before it generates the brief.
  • Risk 3 · No up-to-date information yet. Build 2 uses the free tier of Gemini without web search, to keep the cost at $0 while I compare models. So news and financials are not filled in: the brief says “Not verified” and lists what to look up, instead of inventing them. Next push: choose the most efficient model with web search (Gemini with Google Search is the main option) and add a hard monthly cap on searches and brief calls before any paid limit is reached.

How the app works (Build 2):

  1. Login. The student creates an account with Google (name, email and read-only calendar access, asked once) or logs in. A demo mode with a fictional calendar works without an account.
  2. Calendar. A small local server reads the last 7 days and the next 14 days from Google Calendar (Build 3: the last 4 weeks to 2 weeks ahead). Meeting links, emails and phone numbers are removed before anything is shown or sent to the AI.
  3. Networking filter. Rules first (free and explainable): classes, exams, all-day, declined, recurring and internal events are hidden; coffee chats, panels, info sessions, mixers and interviews are kept. Only unclear events go to the AI, which returns a type, a confidence and a reason. The student can correct the type, and the app remembers it.
  4. Brief. “Generate brief with AI” sends the event details (no emails or links) to Gemini through the local server. The brief has 8 sections and 10 questions, is labeled “AI-generated draft”, never invents news, figures or facts about people, and shows tokens and cost for each run.
  5. Analytics. PostHog records page views, clicks and key actions on this page and in the app, with session replay that masks all text and inputs.

Architecture: [React app in the browser] → [local Node server: holds the keys] → [Google sign-in + Google Calendar API (read-only)] and [Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite] → [brief in the app]. API keys and Google tokens never reach the browser or Git.

4. PRD v3 (Build 3): Hosting, Database, Security and the Core Workflow

Riskiest assumption now: the brief is only worth opening if its facts are current and trustworthy, and the app is only worth keeping if it also handles what happens after the event. Build 3 tests both with real inputs on a live app.

  • Current facts (Risk 3 from PRD v2): briefs are written by a small research agent. It picks the focus area, makes light checks on people (career and interests only) and deep research on up to 4 companies, then runs parallel Google Searches. Every fact gets a number that opens its source, and people are never invented. Result: no more “Not verified” sections for news and financials. Each brief cites about 30–120 sources.
  • After the event: people you met wait in Contacts → To do. You can connect on LinkedIn (search link plus a 300-character note to copy) or by email, or dismiss them. Only people you choose enter your contacts. The app then tracks emails exchanged, the last interaction and follow-up tasks: thank-you after a coffee chat, reply within a day, follow-up after 7 days. Emails are AI drafts that are sent only after you confirm.
  • Finding events: Discover suggests in-person NYC events that fit your goals. They come only from trusted platforms (Eventbrite, Luma, Meetup, read from their own event data) and from club and school newsletters in Gmail. The AI only ranks them; it can't add events or links. This came after a first version where the AI searched the web and invented or mislinked events.

Hosting, database and security choices:

  • Hosting: Render (free web service). One Node server serves the app and the API on one URL, so keys stay on the server. It sleeps after 15 minutes idle (first load is about 50 seconds), which is fine for a class pilot.
  • Database: Supabase Postgres, used only by the server with a secret key. Row Level Security is on for every table with no public policies, so the public key can't read anything. Rows are keyed by a hash of the email. Google tokens and sessions are encrypted (AES-256-GCM).
  • Access control: the account always comes from the server-side session, never from the request. An automated test proves that Account B can't read or overwrite Account A.
  • Spending and abuse: per-visitor rate limits (429), an app-wide hourly ceiling, and a $5 monthly AI budget that also counts paid searches. Gmail sending is limited to 10 per hour and always needs a confirm click.
  • Privacy: emails, links and phone numbers are removed before any AI call. Contacts' email addresses never go to the AI. PostHog session replay masks all text and inputs.
  • Full audit: secret search in the repo and on the live site, npm audit (0 vulnerabilities), and an agent attack review with 17 findings fixed, in SECURITY.md.

Architecture (Build 3): [React app in the browser] → [Node server on Render: holds every key, rate limits, budget] → [Google sign-in + Calendar (read-only) + Gmail (read + drafts)] · [Gemini 3.8 Flash with Google Search for research and writing; Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite for small calls] · [Supabase Postgres, server-only] · [Eventbrite / Luma / Meetup public event pages].

Build 2 · Smallest Local Version & PostHog (Due Nov 16)

  • What shipped: PostHog on this page (clicked_try_product) and in the app (page views, event_opened, brief_generated, signed_in, calendar_connected, event_classified_override…) with masked session replay; a real AI brief with Gemini through a local server (screenshot above); Log in / Create account with Google; read-only Google Calendar; a rules-first networking classifier with AI for unclear events; “Add event” for events the calendar misses; a demo mode with a fictional calendar; PRD v2; setup in AGENTS.md. 69 automated tests pass.
  • What changed in the PRD: the AI brief is generated from the calendar (not a separate page); the brief keeps the same 8 sections as the samples, but news and financials stay “Not verified” until web search is added; the riskiest assumption is now written down with three risks and how each is handled.
  • Problems and how they were fixed:
    • The model wasn't good enough for useful briefs. Even with web search, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite gave generic briefs full of “Not verified”, much weaker than the briefs I make by hand with Claude. Three fixes:
      • A better model for research and writing (Gemini 3.8 Flash). The cheap model is kept for small calls.
      • A research agent instead of one prompt. It plans what to look up (focus area, competitors, up to 4 companies and people chosen by my profile), runs the searches in parallel, retries empty ones, and then writes.
      • More sources per brief: the cap went from 40 to 120, from about 10 sources to 30–120.
      Cost per brief went from ≈ $0.003 to ≈ $0.05.
    • The brief format took several rounds.
      • Web research first appeared as a separate panel, when I wanted it inside the brief.
      • Facts had no visible source, so each fact got a numbered citation.
      • The source list was too long, so each number now opens a small popup with its link.
      • The content still felt thin and repetitive. I shared my own Claude briefs and their method, and the structure changed:
        • Company conclusion first, then the focus area with its own bottom line.
        • Light on people, deep on companies, financials, business bets and industry news.
        • Repeated facts removed, newest news first.
        • A language setting (English or Spanish).
      • Gemini also returned no sources when asked for JSON or tables, so searching and writing became separate steps.
    • The agent once filled an unnamed speaker with a real executive. Fixed with a rule (only people named in the invitation) and a check.
    • Discover needed three versions.
      • The first asked the AI to search the web for events. Most events or links were wrong or invented, some pointed to other events, and it had a refresh button I didn't want.
      • Checking each link was not enough.
      • The final version reads only trusted platforms' own event data (Eventbrite, Luma, Meetup) plus club and school newsletters in Gmail. Filters: in person, NYC, next 2 months, no spam or promoters. The AI only ranks the events.
      • Testing then got Eventbrite to answer “too many requests”, so the app now reads one page at a time and stops on 429.
    • The contacts follow-up took four rounds.
      • It created a thank-you task after every event. Now thank-you notes come only after coffee chats, and LinkedIn needs nothing more.
      • People had to be added by hand. Now people from past networking events are picked up automatically.
      • That made one “connect” task per person, repeated on the calendar, which looked like spam. Now there is one To do list, and one calendar card: “N contacts pending to connect”.
      • People I hadn't decided on still showed up in my contacts. Now they wait in To do (Connect or Dismiss) and only enter my contacts when I connect on LinkedIn or by email. Contacts also show emails exchanged (12 months of Gmail), the last interaction and links to each email.
    • Setting up the cloud took many clicks.
      • Supabase: project, keys and SQL migrations.
      • Render: environment variables and manual deploys. Several “it doesn't show” moments were just a missing deploy.
      • Google Cloud: production redirect URL and the Gmail API. Sign-in was blocked until my accounts were added as test users.
    • Calendar range. Seven days back and 14 ahead was not enough for follow-ups. It now covers the last 4 weeks through 2 weeks ahead, and the last month stays saved.
    • Security review findings. Event links could redirect the server to internal addresses. Now every redirect is checked.
    • A script emptied a code file. Restored from Git, and added as a rule in AGENTS.md.
  • What I learned: how hosting, a database and row-level security fit together; why the browser should never talk to the database; how OAuth testing mode, scopes and test users work; that an AI that “searches the web” still needs real sources it can't make up; and to audit security on every delivery.
  • Cost & time this week: ≈ $0.84 (Gemini; Render, Supabase and PostHog free) · 5–6 hours
